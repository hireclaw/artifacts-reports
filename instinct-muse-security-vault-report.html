Part 1 — Instinct: the Vault and the 1Password move DEEP DIVE

1.1 What Instinct is (official facts)

Company & product

  • Operated by Spear Street Technology, Inc. (San Francisco), per its own terms and California filings.
  • Founded/led by Noah Shinn, former research scientist at Sierra.
  • Official site instinct.co: "a personal assistant that understands what you're working on… there are no new interfaces. It's trained to use a phone and a computer. You can text or call it."
  • Connects to email, messaging, calendar, screen, audio, location and more; handles bookings, rides, inbox cleanup, shopping, subscriptions.
  • Private access only, scaling compute; waitlist at instinct.co/login.

Funding (reported)

  • $250M Series B at a $2.5B valuation, led by Index Ventures and Benchmark — shared by Instinct with the WSJ (Aug 2026).
  • Kleiner Perkins and Conviction invested in earlier rounds that "have now closed" (TechCrunch, from multiple investors).
  • Viral trajectory often compared to OpenClaw and Poke in the 2026 personal-agent wave.

1.2 The Vault — Instinct's built-in credential store

Instinct's answer to "an agent needs to log in as you" is a feature literally called the Vault: users hand it credentials for services the agent operates on their behalf — loyalty programs, streaming and software subscriptions, reservation systems, healthcare portals, payroll services. Per Noah Shinn's own post, 32% of Instinct users already share credentials through it.

"For Materials that you add to our feature of the Service known as the 'Vault' (or its successor name), we will only use such Materials to provide the Services to you and will not use such Materials to train AI models." — Instinct Terms of Service (instinct.co/terms), official, retrieved Sep 16, 2026

That is a real, meaningful commitment — inside terms that are much broader everywhere else. The same ToS grants Instinct a "perpetual and irrevocable" license to your other materials (including for AI training, with an opt-out at app.instinct.com/settings), describes receiving screen captures, cursor movements and keyboard inputs from your devices, and makes the user warrant they have the right to hand over "any payment method, account credential, password or other access method." Instinct also states it "make[s] no representation or warranty that such safeguards will prevent unintended or erroneous Actions" — you remain responsible for safeguards on each connected service.

1.3 The 1Password partnership — brokering credentials instead of storing them

On Sep 4, 2026, Noah Shinn announced on X that Instinct is partnering with 1Password so users can share account credentials through a vault — 1Password acting as a credential broker — "rather than only relying on the Vault it built itself." The integration is rolling out to Instinct's early-access group.

The mechanism maps onto 1Password's officially published "Unified Access" platform (launched Mar 17, 2026). Its official architecture makes three claims, quoted from 1Password's own post:

⏱ Just-in-time delivery
Credentials are provided "at the moment they're needed, evaluating access in context when it's requested" — not as long-lived secrets the agent holds continuously.
🧾 Unified audit trails
A single system of record shows "which credential was used, by whom or what, and when" — across human and agent identities.
🔒 No secret exposure
Teams can "take ownership of a credential and enable its use without ever exposing the secret itself" to the requester.
⚠ What is NOT confirmed
No technical spec for the Instinct integration exists. Whether Instinct's agent process ever touches plaintext is unpublished. Instinct was not among the original Unified Access launch partners (Anthropic, OpenAI, Cursor, GitHub, Vercel, CoreWeave, Perplexity…).

Context matters: this move follows real damage. Testers found Instinct kept emails in plaintext after access was revoked, could be phished, and sent email without checking. A brokered, scoped, audited credential path is exactly the fix security researchers asked for — but as of today it is an announcement plus 1Password's platform architecture, not a verified Instinct implementation.

Part 2 — Meta Muse: Secure VM, Sentinel, surrogation DEEP DIVE

2.1 The official architecture diagram (from Meta)

Published by Meta in "How We Built Safety Into Muse" (research.meta.ai, Sep 8, 2026). Downloaded unmodified from Meta's servers — source page.

2.2 Muse Secure VM — two isolated security domains on one box

You and your Muse share your own dedicated computer in the cloud — the system of record for your data and credentials. Meta's stated mental model: "two isolated security domains on one box, not an LLM powered agent with root."

DomainWhat runs there and why
Runtime cell (untrusted)The Hatch daemon (Meta's internal codename for Muse), your workspace filesystem, and every binary/tool Muse executes — inside a systemd-nspawn container. Cell root maps to an unprivileged host user; own root filesystem (full Debian image); virtual network interface; filtered syscalls (no io_uring); stripped kernel capabilities (no CAP_SYS_PTRACE, no CAP_NET_ADMIN). This is the only part that touches untrusted content.
Host-side services (trusted)Separate systemd units, deliberately outside the runtime cell because the cell is expected to process hostile data — attackers inside the cell cannot disable them:
  • hatch-safety — an independent set of models/classifiers inspecting every request and response to/from core model inference (frontier-risk control + prompt-injection detection). Outside the cell ⇒ can't be turned off by the agent.
  • privsep — workers that execute built-in connector code with tightly scoped privileges, keeping connector credentials out of the agent's scope entirely.
  • hatch-authd — credential storage (OAuth tokens for connected services live in your VM, not centralized Meta infrastructure) and credential surrogation, so the main agent never sees sensitive credentials.
  • Sentinel — the sole permission authority for connector actions and all network egress (next section).
  • Durable app state in a Postgres database separate from both the runtime cell and the credential store; constrained proxies for inference and telemetry.
  • Cell ⇄ services communication only via Unix domain sockets with SO_PEERCRED + peer ACLs — kernel-authenticated, least-privilege IPC "with no secrets to steal."

2.3 Sentinel — the gate nothing bypasses

Sentinel is a separate host-side agent from Muse. Muse proposes actions; only Sentinel grants them. Two chokepoints:

Connector actions

When Muse wants a connector action, the tool call in the runtime cell submits a request to Sentinel describing the connector, method, action class, scope and the user's original ask — Sentinel turns that into a user-visible purpose, checks the user's connector policy, and decides: allowed / denied / ask.

Network egress + just-in-time credential insertion

  • All runtime traffic goes through a forward proxy wired up with Linux networking controls (userns, veth boundaries, eBPF filtering). Sentinel inspects L4 + L7: hostname, resolved and final IP, port, protocol, HTTP method, path, and the actual decoded request. SSRF restrictions stop "public" hostnames resolving to private infrastructure.
  • Surrogate tokens: code in the runtime cell only ever sees a "surrogate" token minted by authd. After a request is authorized, Sentinel swaps the surrogate for the real credential at the network boundary. Meta's own words: the agent never sees real tokens, "which means any attempt to coerce the agent to reveal the actual secrets via prompt-injection or otherwise is futile."
  • Tainted egress: kernel-level data-flow tracking. Each tool process starts clean and becomes tainted if it reads user data. Clean processes can use a narrow auto-allow policy; tainted or unverifiable ones lose auto-allow and fall back to human approval. Implemented with eBPF cgroup programs + eBPF on Linux Security Module hooks for taint propagation.

2.4 Human in the loop — approvals as strict capabilities

When Sentinel resolves to ask, execution stops and a pending approval goes straight to the Muse client — the dialog appears in the app UI, not in the chat with Muse, and the answer is routed directly back to Sentinel. Approvals are "strict capabilities, not conversational suggestions," bound to a particular connector/destination/use case, with grant types: one-time, session-scoped, task-scoped, time-bounded, or perpetual. Read-only, previously allowed, or demonstrably low-risk actions flow without interruption — friction only where consent matters.

2.5 The credential vault itself — authd in your VM

  • Storage location: credentials and OAuth tokens live in a separate isolated container inside your own VM — "not in other Meta services," not centralized Meta infrastructure. Continuously backed up for restore.
  • Least privilege everywhere: read/write access split per service; fine-grained controls beyond OAuth scopes (e.g., grant Gmail read but strip settings access); controls at connector, process, credential and request levels. "The model doesn't need to see the API keys — so it doesn't."
  • privsep split (the three-way rule): privsep decides where credential-capable code executes · authd decides which credential material a caller can receive · Sentinel decides whether the action may be taken. Each worker is identified by its cgroup with explicit credential allowlists — a calendar worker can't fetch an email credential by tweaking a parameter.
  • Email hardening: the email connector filters out one-time passcodes, password-reset links and login magic links (deterministic filters + a classifier) — connecting your inbox must not hand the agent the keys to reset your whole life.

2.6 Browser and purchases

Browser with a virtualization layer

  • Real up-to-date Chromium; users can watch and take over at any time.
  • Website logins: a custom client UI captures username/password and routes them directly to authd — stored outside the runtime cell, never visible to the main agent, and injected into the browser page at the point of need (agent is paused during fills and during user takeover).
  • The browser sub-agent sees only an accessibility-tree snapshot — not the raw DOM, so it cannot read filled credentials. No JavaScript execution in page context, no script verbs, no exec in the browser process, Chrome devtools disabled. CDP access sits with a broker outside the runtime cell.
  • A family of independent classifiers watches for: personal-data egress unrelated to the task, prompt injection in page DOM, in images/media, in downloaded files, and high-risk form submissions — blocking or forcing user review. Meta's existing malicious-site blocklists are matched inside the VM too.

Purchases (money is treated as the highest-risk path)

  • Known-site checkout: browser detects the checkout page → human-in-the-loop approval with the exact purchase details, every time.
  • New sites: Muse's wallet (partnered with Stripe Link at launch, Shop Pay coming) issues a single-use card number tied to that merchant, that dollar amount, valid for a limited period — "even if it were stolen via prompt injection… it would not be particularly useful to the attacker."

2.7 Defense in depth against prompt injection

Meta builds around Simon Willison's "lethal trifecta" (private data access + untrusted content + ability to communicate externally). Four layers, plus deterministic boundaries underneath:

  1. Model level — Muse Spark 1.3 is trained to recognize/resist injection ("close to SOTA" on their evals).
  2. Harness level — all external data entering context is labeled untrusted.
  3. Classifier ensemble — multiple injection-detection classifiers trained on real datasets + scaled agentic red-teaming, run in parallel on everything entering context via files and tool calls; trained independently from the model for higher combined accuracy.
  4. Human approvals — on anything that moves data out of the VM.

Beneath the agent, deterministic limits hold even if the model misbehaves: runtime cell limits system access · privsep restricts which code sees which credentials · authd applies ACLs · Sentinel evaluates every action and all egress.

2.8 Bug bounty, Confidential VM, data policy

Bug bounty (open to anyone)

Up to $300,000 for valid reports at bugbounty.meta.com — including up to $130,000 for a successful prompt injection affecting one user. Continuous agentic red-teaming feeds offline evals.

Coming: Muse Confidential VM

Later in 2026, Meta plans a mode that cryptographically and verifiably prevents even Meta from accessing data in your VM — design and source already shared with external auditors, continuous public audit promised once live.

Data policy (official)

  • No sharing of conversations or VM data with Meta ad systems.
  • Nuance: web browsing appears as your activity, so merchant visits can indirectly influence ads you see elsewhere.
  • Inference trajectories are sanitized of PII before use in training; full opt-out switch in settings.
  • At launch Meta can still access data when necessary to support/secure/operate the service — exactly what Confidential VM is meant to remove.
