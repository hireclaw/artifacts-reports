Two Suns,
One Clock.

The sun lifts over Arunachal Pradesh while Gujarat is still dark — nearly two hours apart, inside one country, on one clock. This is the story of why India keeps a single time… and what would happen if it kept two.

Chapter 01 · How time is made

The world agreed to count hours from a line in Greenwich.

GMT is where time begins — zero degrees longitude. Every other place is simply hours added or subtracted from it. India chose its meridian at 82.5° East and set IST, Indian Standard Time, at GMT + 5 hours 30 minutes.

Chapter 02 · The puzzle

India stretches across 29 degrees of longitude.

That is a two-hour spread of solar time between the eastern dawn and the western dusk — compressed into a single zone. The east loses sunlight in the morning; the west keeps it late at night. One clock, two very different mornings.

Chapter 03 · The forgotten clock

Assam once ran on tea time.

Before the clocks were standardised, colonial Assam kept its own chaibagaan — tea garden time, set to GMT + 5:54. Planters wanted the day to start with the sun, not with the capital. It was the east's quiet answer to a one-size clock.

After Independence, in 1947, India folded every local time into one IST. The sun rising two hours early in the east became somebody else's arithmetic.

Chapter 04 · What two zones would fix

Mornings that begin with light, not lamps.

A second zone — roughly an hour ahead for the northeast — would let the day begin when the sun does. Early natural light means less electricity burned before offices open, safer winter mornings for schoolchildren in the east, and work hours that match daylight instead of fighting it.

Chapter 05 · What it would break

But a clock is also a treaty.

Two zones would fix

  • Sunrise misalignment in the northeast
  • Wasted evening daylight in the west
  • Heavy morning power demand

Two zones would break

  • Rail & flight timetables overnight
  • Offices, markets, exams, broadcasting
  • A seam down the middle of one identity

Trains crossing the seam, stock exchanges, television schedules, national exams — every shared moment of a billion people runs on the fact that everyone agrees on what "now" means.

Chapter 06 · The bottom line

One sun sweeps this country. One people keep one time. The question is not whether two dawns exist — they already do — but whether one clock must pretend they don't.

So the clock stays, for now: a single, stubborn, remarkably useful fiction — and one of the quiet things that holds a huge, restless country together.
