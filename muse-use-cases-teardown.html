03 · Method

The guide's own playbook

The cases are the bait; the transferable asset is the method section. The author wraps every case in a master prompt, extracts 12 reusable patterns, and hard-codes guardrails.

The master prompt (verbatim)

I want you to run this real Muse use case for me, end to end: [PASTE ONE CASE PROMPT FROM BELOW] Ask me for any missing details up front, in one round. Then execute without stopping. Only hand control back to me where a human decision, identity check, signature, payment, or approval is genuinely required. Verify the outcome and report what you did when it’s done.

12 reusable patterns

“Most ‘new’ use cases are combinations of these loops.”

Discover → reconcile → resolve
Search across inboxes and accounts, cross-check what happened, then close the loop.
Monitor → detect → act
Watch prices, seats, sales, deadlines, or inventory; alert or act when the condition hits.
Collect → compare → recommend
Gather constrained options, explain the trade-offs, and take the approved path.
Retrieve → apply → transact
Find credits, points, receipts, or benefits, then use them in a real transaction.
Evidence → claim → archive
Assemble proof, submit the form, and preserve confirmations for later.
Extract → transform → share
Turn messy portals, menus, posts, or screenshots into a clean accessible artifact.
Bulk cleanup → rules → log
Apply a repeatable rule across many items and leave a change log.
Inbox/calendar → obligation → reminder
Infer the real task from incoming information and put it on the right timeline.
Generate → iterate → package
Build a dashboard, app, game, playlist, or guide and refine it into a usable artifact.
Conversation → browser → completion
Use chat as the control plane for a real workflow across websites and apps.
Approval-first execution
Let Muse do the reversible work; keep payments, signatures, and sensitive choices human.
Persistent context → less re-explaining
Carry preferences, history, and family context forward so each run starts ahead.

“Run it with guardrails” — six caveats

“These caveats appeared repeatedly in hands-on reports. Build them into your prompt.”

CaveatAdvice
Monitoring can fail silentlyFor long-running watches, ask for heartbeat checks, failure alerts, and a final proof-of-state.
Connected services can dropTell Muse where to resume, and request a count of completed vs. remaining items.
Ambitious creative work needs iterationAsk for a first draft, inspect it, then give concrete corrections rather than one giant prompt.
Verify factual outputFor claims, bookings, money, and identity, require source links, receipts, or confirmation numbers.
Keep approvals explicitPayments, signatures, sends, and account changes should pause at the real decision point.
Scope mattersSay what Muse may read, change, send, or buy—and what stays off-limits.

Reading one — life admin dominates. 69% of cases sit in five admin-heavy categories, and booking/appointments appear in nearly 1 in 5 cases. People are delegating logistics, not idle chat.
Reading two — the agent as advocate. Money alone is 100 cases: refund escalation (“escalate past the bot until you reach a human”), insurance quote fights, promised credits, ISP outages, double-charges. A recurring prompt style — found in 145 of 500 prompts (29%) — explicitly demands verification and a final report: the community has already learned agents drift and bake guardrails into their prompts.
Reading three — agents that build. 65 cases are “build me…”: photo calorie trackers replacing paid subscriptions, custom Notion connectors, a todo-sync for Things.app “which has no official API.” The expectation is an agent that constructs its own tooling, not just clicks links.
Reading four — creators are here. 53 cases involve posting/scheduling content or running channels; Work's examples read like an ops team in one thread (“found all the videos he forgot to post, then scheduled them all”).
