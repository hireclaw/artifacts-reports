Needle 3: an 8–29 MB model
that acts, not chats

Cactus Compute shipped a foundation model for tiny devices — one set of weights where every depth from 2 to 20 layers is a deployable model. Tool calls, structured extraction and embeddings, fully offline, on watches, robots and microcontrollers. This is how it works, what it claims, and what to make of it.

The one-paragraph version

Needle 3 (released Sep 17 2026) is a foundation model built for acting on-device — not conversation. It reads tool definitions or a schema, then returns structured JSON: which function to call with which arguments, or typed fields extracted from messy text. Cactus trades away general chat ability to make a single 8–29 MB binary that, by their measurements, beats models 10× its size on mobile tool-calling suites and matches 2–3× bigger models on extraction — all running offline on a Raspberry Pi 5, a phone, or a watch.

Skill 1

Tool calls

Given your app's functions, Needle picks the right ones and fills every argument from the user's words. Two asks → two calls, in order. Unknown ask → an empty list, not a hallucinated guess.

Skill 2

Structured extraction

Declare a shape (even a Pydantic model), hand over messy text, get typed fields back. The decode grammar guarantees the output parses. Generalises to classification.

Skill 3

Text embedding

The same model emits sentence vectors — local semantic search, query→tool matching, near-duplicate alert merging. Nothing leaves the device.

Why this matters in 2026: the personal-agent wave keeps hitting the same wall — phones can't run frontier models, and cloud round-trips cost privacy, latency and battery. Needle 3 attacks the exact gap: doing actions offline (dim lights, read a card charge, steer a robot), not chatting. Infrastructure for the "agent on the wrist / in the vacuum" era.

Three ideas hold it up

1 · SAN→delete the FFN, keep attention→context-grounded, tiny
2 · Intelligence ladder→one training run→19 nested subnetworks→pick size at deploy time
3 · Confidence gating→calibrated score per call→suppress below floor→route on the number

Each gets its own tab below — with the actual maths and numbers from Cactus's two research posts (both dated Sep 17 2026, alongside an arXiv paper).

The intelligence ladder

Normally you'd train a family of models — small for the watch, big for the phone — and maintain each separately. Needle 3 trains one 20-layer stack such that 19 nested subnetworks (depths 2–20) are each a working model sharing the same weights. Pick your size at deploy time. Same engine, same file.

Every subnetwork keeps block 0 and block 19 (endpoints, black) and fills the interior by repeatedly halving the widest gap: order 0, 19, 9, 14, 4, 6, 11, 16, 2, 7, 12, 17, 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, 13, 15, 18. Subnetwork d = the first d blocks of that order — each depth is a strict superset of the one below, so shallow subnetworks still see early and late computation.

Sizes interpolate between Cactus's anchors (8 MB @ 4L → 29 MB @ 20L); below 4 layers the tied embedding table dominates, so the slider floors there. Skipping a block is the identity on the residual stream — and the KV cache is per-block, so shallower = proportionally smaller cache, which is what memory-bound devices actually feel.

How it's trained without training 19 models

  • Sampled paths: each step runs the full model with probability 0.8 (ordinary LM step), or a random depth d ∈ {2…19} with probability 0.2, with the loss scaled by d/20.
  • Self-distillation: on subnetwork steps, a KL term (λ=1, stop-gradient) pulls the shallow pass toward the frozen full model's predictions — recovering gradient the shallow rung alone couldn't carry, at no extra forward pass.
  • Free regularisation: in matched pairs, the ladder-trained net hit 1.958 validation loss at full depth vs 2.037 without the ladder — and its 16-layer exit matched the control's full 18 layers. No block can become load-bearing when every prefix must work.
  • Multi-lane residual: 4 parallel residual streams on a round-robin lane schedule, renumbered inside each subnetwork so surviving blocks still alternate lanes. Engram memory sites and global-attention layers stay attached to their blocks.

The slice-of-slice trap (honestly documented)

A sliced model has its own block numbering — re-slice it by its numbering and you get a different, untrained set of blocks. Of the 14 subnetworks of a 16-layer slice, 13 differed from the parent's. The fix: every slice carries its parent's selection order, so slicing a slice equals slicing the original. A subtle deployment bug they chose to write down — a good sign.

Simple Attention Networks — the research bet

Needle 3's size and speed come from deleting the feed-forward network — two-thirds of a transformer's non-embedding parameters — from every block. Cactus published a controlled study (arXiv 2607.18363) asking what that actually costs.

Standard block

x → Attention → FFN (SwiGLU) → x
     ~⅓ of weights   ~⅔ of weights
     "routing"       "memory / storage"

The FFN is the model's key-value store of training facts.

SAN block (Needle)

x → Attention → x
   pre-norm · GQA · RoPE
   QK-norm · scalar residual gate
   4-lane residual · tied embeddings

Selects and transports content already in context — cannot synthesise what the context doesn't support. "Context-grounded."

Method hygiene worth noting: per-arm learning-rate sweeps (the optimal Muon rate differs 2× between arms), a pipeline noise floor of 0.0015 nats, pre-registered predictions, and gap shrinkage with training (0.046 → 0.019 → 0.0055 nats at 5B / 30B / 105B tokens).

Where the deficit lives — and why it's fine for Needle

  • The gap is concentrated on query tokens — positions where context offers nothing to route from. On traces and answer regions the SAN leads; it even reverses Lambada (0.203 vs 0.181) when trained on distribution-matched web text.
  • Translation: FFNs are storage; attention is routing. Tool calling and extraction are routing problems — the function names, argument values and schema are all in the prompt. That is exactly Needle's regime.
  • Needle buys back the query-token deficit with engram memory — memory sites bolted onto specific blocks, inherited from Needle 2.
  • Training dynamics: routing matrices (Q, K) freeze in the first quarter of training; content-writing matrices keep gaining rank forever — in SANs that role moves to the attention output projection, the only write path left.
  • Ablations: QK-norm is load-bearing (removing it diverges — the study's only divergence); residual scalar gates are performance-neutral but diagnostic; post-attention sandwich norm beats baseline by 0.009 nats; 20 layers is the optimum at matched params; 48-layer SAN stacks train without incident. Every instability in the study happened in the FFN arm.
The honest trade: at matched parameters a SAN pays ~2× the FLOPs per token at 2048 context. The trade wins where memory, not compute, is the binding constraint — which is precisely the on-device regime. Cactus states it as a regime claim, not superiority. MMLU-class benchmarks are at chance at these scales; this is not a knowledge model.

Text embedding, the quiet third skill

Same model, different head: sentence vectors for on-device semantic search, routing a query to the right tool, or de-duplicating alerts ("drone battery" vs "battery of drone" match). At these sizes the vectors are coarse, but for match/rank/route inside one app's vocabulary, local beats nothing-available.

The privacy angle, made concrete

A card-charge reader, a health-data parser, a home controller — the classic objection is "I'm sending my raw data to a cloud model to get JSON back." Needle 3's pitch: the model is small enough to live next to the data, so the sensitive text never crosses a network. That's a compliance-friendly story (and a battery-friendly one: no radio wake-ups for routine commands).

The developer path

Define tools → run the loop

# pip install cactus-toolkit
from cactus import Needle, tool
needle = Needle("path/to/needle3.cact")

@tool(description="Dim the lights in a specific room.")
def dim_light(room: str, brightness: int = 30):
    ...

@tool(description="Toggle the lock state of a door.")
def toggle_lock(location: str):
    ...

result = needle.run("Dim the bedroom and lock up")
# → calls dim_light, then toggle_lock, results attached

The decorator + docstring/description is the whole tool interface — Needle reads descriptions literally.

Extraction

from pydantic import BaseModel

class CardCharge(BaseModel):
    merchant: str
    amount: float

needle.extract(
    "Rs 1,250 at BLU SMART on Tue",
    schema=CardCharge
)

Regex triggers (gate bypass)

# user speech starting "hey assistant"
# is FORCED to route to wake_tool,
# confidence gate skipped:
triggers = ["(?i)^hey assistant.*"]

needle.run(
    user_input, trigger_patterns=triggers
)

Tool-writing rules (the part that actually determines quality)

  • Keep each tool's description narrow and plain — no marketing copy, no API docs.
  • One tool per action: don't register "manage smart home"; register set_light_brightness and toggle_lock.
  • Parameter names should mirror user words — the model fills arguments by matching the sentence to names.
  • Use enum values that look like user phrases, where possible.
  • Make optional arguments truly optional with defaults; put value formats in descriptions.
  • Keep the toolset small — every tool you add is another thing the model can confuse.

Fine-tune + slice (one adapter, every depth)

# train a LoRA adapter at the full 20 layers...
needle finetune data.jsonl --epochs 10 --out adapter.safetensors

# ...merge, then slice to the depth your device wants.
# The adapter trains ALL subnetworks at once (weights are shared).
needle build --lora adapter.safetensors --layers 8 \
             --platform linux-arm64 --out ./pi

Tuning lifts every depth 18–36 points on DroidCall; from 4 layers up, tuned subnetworks pass DeepSeek V4 Flash on Cactus's suites. One adapter serves every rung — no per-size training.

Confidence as an API

The gate is a calibrated head, floor default 0.1. Suppressed calls aren't errors — they're a signal: route them to a cloud model, a confirmation UI, or a retry with clarification. That's the recommended architecture: device-first, escalate only when unsure.

The verdict

What's genuinely strong
  • The ladder is a real idea, not marketing: one weights file → 19 deployable models, with measured self-regularisation benefits (1.958 vs 2.037 val loss) and per-block KV cache scaling. It kills the "train a family of models" maintenance problem.
  • Research is unusually transparent for a product launch: a controlled ablation study (arXiv), pre-registered predictions, a published negative result (2-layer generalist is useless), and a self-documented slicing bug. That's more rigour than most model releases.
  • The niche is correct: tool calling + extraction are routing problems, and the SAN paper argues exactly why FFN-free models fit them. "Beats models 10× its size" is scoped to that regime — and at 8–29 MB, "10×" models are still small ones, so read the claim carefully.
  • Deployment surface is serious: 11 platforms, sub-1-MB engines, microcontrollers to phones, WASM included.
What to discount or verify
  • Every benchmark is vendor-run on vendor-built suites (DroidCall 200 rows, Mobile Actions 961 rows) against DSV4 Flash over an API. No independent replication yet.
  • No chat, no knowledge: MMLU-class performance is at chance at this scale — by design. If your assistant needs to answer questions, this is a component, not the brain. Pair it with a cloud LLM for reasoning.
  • FLOPs cost: ~2× compute per token at matched parameters — fine when memory-bound, a real cost when tokens are many.
  • Per-request depth switching not shipped yet — the coolest capability is currently a promise.
  • Confidence floor 0.1 is low; you'll want to tune the gate per product, and their own 2-layer gated score of ~0 shows the gate can erase a model entirely.
Fit with the broader trend: 2026's agent stack is consolidating into "big cloud brain + tiny local hands." Needle 3 is a credible candidate for the local hands: action routing offline, structured output by grammar, embeddings included. For anyone building voice-controlled devices, IoT products or privacy-sensitive phone agents, this is one of the most interesting small-model releases of the year. For chat products — wrong tool, deliberately.
